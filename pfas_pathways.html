Landfill gas carries volatile PFAS into the air — and we're barely measuring it

As waste breaks down underground, it produces landfill gas — primarily methane, but also volatile compounds including fluorotelomer alcohols (FTOHs) and other air-loving PFAS.

Modern landfills burn this gas at flares to manage methane. But standard flares don't reliably destroy PFAS — the chemicals can survive combustion and escape into the atmosphere.

Recent research suggests PFAS leaving through gas may be comparable to or greater than what leaves through leachate — yet there is almost no routine air monitoring around landfills. We don't know where it travels or who is breathing it.

Rainwater dissolves PFAS from buried waste and carries it to our waterways

As rainwater and moisture from decomposition percolate through buried waste, they dissolve water-soluble PFAS like PFOA and PFOS — from food packaging, carpet, raingear, and thousands of other everyday products.

This liquid — leachate — collects above the liner and is piped or trucked to wastewater treatment plants not equipped to remove PFAS. The chemicals pass through intact and are discharged into rivers and lakes.

In some cases, PFAS levels in treated effluent are actually higher than in incoming sewage. In 2024, 36 New York plants processed more than 1 million gallons of PFAS-laden leachate every single day.
